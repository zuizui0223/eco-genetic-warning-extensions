# Urban–island literature synthesis for functional fragmentation

## Claim discipline

The present model does **not** show that urban and island systems occupy the same functional-fragmentation regime. The defensible cross-system statement is prospective:

> Urban and island landscapes provide contrasting causal decompositions with which to test whether different fragmentation mechanisms can converge on the same operational functional-fragmentation regime.

The operational regime is defined by state establishment, realised function, loss incidence/reproducibility, biological connectivity and genetic state—not by the labels `urban` or `island` and not by one network metric.

This note separates three levels: **established external evidence**, **implications of the present finite results**, and **future convergence hypotheses**.

## 1. Established external evidence

### 1.1 Spatial fragmentation does not imply one interaction-network response

Recent synthesis strongly rejects a one-dimensional `fragmentation -> network simplification` rule. A global meta-analysis of anthropogenic effects on plant–pollinator networks found reduced plant/pollinator richness and changes in nestedness/specialisation, but connectance and modularity were often unchanged and responses were heterogeneous. A 2025 plant–vertebrate pollinator study separated habitat amount from fragmentation and found that habitat loss, not fragmentation per se, predicted richness and structural change; about 90% of interaction dissimilarity between strongly contrasting landscapes was attributed to species turnover, while rewiring mattered more when species pools overlapped. A 2025 global meta-analysis of insect pollinators likewise found negative fragmentation associations but identified reduced habitat area as the strongest component.

**Implication for this study:** define functional fragmentation by loss or destabilisation of effective interaction-supported function, not by connectance, partner richness or patchiness alone.

### 1.2 Network structure and functional identity can matter beyond species counts

Empirical and theoretical work increasingly separates species richness from interaction architecture and partner function. Across 40 coastal plant–pollinator networks, loss of pollinator functional diversity—not pollinator species diversity itself—reduced community-wide trait matching and pollination success. Empirical plant–pollinator network structure has also predicted temporal persistence. Multi-habitat landscapes can show higher interaction evenness, complementarity, robustness and pollination success than expected by simply summing component habitat webs. Recent reviews emphasize that rewiring changes both topology and interaction strength and can alter resilience.

**Implication for this study:** the Phase-F scalar `kappa` result cannot stand in for partner identity, contribution concentration, functional redundancy or rewiring. These are separate causal coordinates.

### 1.3 Urban systems decouple spatial patchiness, interaction turnover and gene flow

Urban interaction ecology is not a simple isolation gradient. A 2025 framework treats urbanization as consecutive filters on species presence, co-occurrence and relationships. In Bengaluru, plant–pollinator interaction turnover varied more with season and local urbanization than with spatial distance, indicating strong environmental filtering; interaction rewiring contributed substantially to temporal/spatial interaction beta diversity. Reviews of urban pollination likewise report strong context dependence rather than a single monotone urban effect.

Crucially for the eco-genetic interpretation, a 2026 review of urban plant population genetics found that urban plant populations often retain genetic diversity comparable to non-urban populations and that gene flow can remain high despite urban fragmentation through long-distance and anthropogenic dispersal. Cities therefore should not be treated as isolated genetic islands.

**Implication for this study:** an urban population can plausibly remain genetically connected while its realised interaction structure or ecological function changes. Neutral-genetic isolation should not be used as a proxy for functional isolation.

### 1.4 Island systems combine physical isolation with mutualist and reproductive filters

Oceanic-island pollination networks are, on average, smaller and lower in interaction diversity than mainland or continental-island networks, but island area alone does not explain every network metric. More recent work shows that island spatial network structure reflects both regional species turnover and local interaction structure; in the Canary Islands, species turnover rather than rewiring was the main regional driver of distance decay in modular structure.

A 2024 global analysis found that mutualist-associated plants are disproportionately underrepresented on oceanic islands and argued that a mutualism filter explains variation in island species deficits beyond classic area/isolation effects. This result has received a published statistical critique and author response, so it is best used as evidence that mutualist availability is a serious candidate filter rather than as an uncontested universal law.

Reproductive assurance adds another decoupling. Baker's-law theory predicts an establishment advantage for uniparental reproduction under mate or pollinator limitation, but island floras do not universally collapse to autonomous selfing. Breeding system and mutualist availability should therefore be measured, not inferred from geography.

**Implication for this study:** geographic isolation alone should not determine an island's expected functional-loss regime. Mutualist availability, interaction identity, reproductive assurance and connectivity are distinct coordinates.

### 1.5 Genetic structure is not a universal readout of habitat fragmentation

A global meta-analysis of fine-scale plant genetic structure found no consistent overall difference among fragmented, degraded and undisturbed habitats; seed-dispersal vector explained variation and effect sizes were highly heterogeneous. Together with the urban genetics literature, this supports a key measurement rule: spatial fragmentation, gene flow and functional interaction support must be estimated separately.

## 2. What the present results actually imply in nature

### Result A — fragmentation can reduce interaction-supported function before population disappearance

The strongest empirical implication is not that every fragmented system should lose network complexity. It is that conservation monitoring should include a **functional endpoint**—for example compatible pollen delivery, seed set attributable to a focal pollinator guild, dispersal effectiveness or another realised service—because presence/abundance can remain positive after function becomes weak.

### Result B — source establishment and post-establishment loss are different stages

Phase F retained R4 across `kappa=3.0–6.0` while source/baseline eligibility varied. In natural systems this predicts that interaction change may first be visible as inability to establish or maintain a functional state, rather than as a changed early-warning lead time. Urban species filters and island mutualist filters are therefore especially relevant to the **source/function boundary**.

### Result C — connectivity can change loss-regime reproducibility without a universal rescue sign

Phase E is allele-frequency mixing only, so it cannot be translated directly into seed, pollen or pollinator dispersal. Its real implication is conceptual: connectivity should be treated as a condition that can change stochastic histories and among-population heterogeneity, not automatically as rescue. Empirical studies should distinguish pollen-mediated gene flow, seed/propagule movement, demographic recolonisation and pollinator movement.

### Result D — warning requires an independently evaluable loss process

The practical implication is a sampling design. Before asking whether genetic change predicts loss, replicate populations or population-years should first be classified by realised functional risk and reproducibility. Genetic lead time is interpretable only after that upstream event-generating process has been defined independently of the warning statistic.

## 3. Prospective urban–island convergence hypothesis

The next cross-system question is deliberately **not** `city versus island`:

> Do distinct fragmentation mechanisms map onto the same operational state once local habitat support, realised interactions, functional redundancy, biological connectivity, reproductive assurance and realised function are measured separately?

Possible routes are different:

- **Urban route:** habitat/matrix filtering + pollution/heat/disturbance + partner turnover/rewiring + corridors and anthropogenic movement.
- **Island route:** colonisation and area/isolation filters + mutualist availability + species turnover + stepping-stone connectivity + reproductive assurance.

Convergence would mean that these different causal routes produce similar combinations of (i) functional-state feasibility, (ii) realised functional level, and (iii) loss incidence/reproducibility under comparable observation windows. It does **not** require similar network topology, similar genetic differentiation or similar geography.

This is a testable future hypothesis, not a conclusion of the current simulation.

## 4. Measurement design implied by the literature and model

For both systems, retain separate measurements of:

1. spatial support: habitat amount, patch geometry, matrix resistance;
2. interaction support: partner identity, visitation/interaction strength and effective partner function;
3. architecture: contribution evenness, functional diversity, specialisation and rewiring;
4. biological connectivity: pollen, seed/propagule, demographic and partner movement where relevant;
5. reproductive assurance / alternative functional routes;
6. realised function through time;
7. genetic state through time.

The shared comparison is then a **regime map**, not a habitat-category contrast.
