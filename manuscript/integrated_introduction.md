# Introduction

Ecological deterioration can remove a population's ecological function before it removes the population itself. Species may remain present, and alleles associated with a functional state may persist, while pollination, seed dispersal, defence, mutualism, or another interaction-dependent role falls below an ecologically effective level. This distinction between numerical persistence and functional persistence has long been recognised in work on ecological and functional extinction, but it is still weakly integrated with genetic monitoring. Conservation assessments commonly track abundance, occupancy, effective population size, connectivity, heterozygosity, or allelic diversity, yet these state variables are not interchangeable with realised ecological function.

Early-warning theory offers a way to turn monitoring from retrospective diagnosis into prospective intervention. Systems approaching some critical transitions may show reduced resilience, altered recovery, rising autocorrelation or variance, spatial reorganisation, or related precursors (Scheffer et al. 2009; Drake & Griffen 2010; Carpenter et al. 2011; Scheffer et al. 2012). The same literature also shows why generic warning claims require caution: abrupt shifts can occur without canonical warning, and indicator performance depends on transition mechanism, noise, observation design, event definition, and statistical selection (Hastings & Wysham 2010; Boettiger & Hastings 2012, 2013). The relevant management question is therefore not whether one statistic universally predicts collapse, but under what biological closure a defined signal precedes a defined undesirable event with enough frequency and lead time to be useful.

Genetic monitoring is a natural candidate for such prospective use because genetic variation responds to effective population size, drift, migration, inbreeding, and selection, often on scales that differ from census abundance. Conservation genetics and genomics have established the management relevance of temporal changes in genetic diversity, effective population size, connectivity, and adaptive variation (Schwartz et al. 2007; Allendorf et al. 2010; Stange et al. 2021; Hogg et al. 2024). However, genetic diversity is usually analysed as a population state, risk correlate, or retrospective indicator. Few studies define a genetic warning event and a later ecological-function event on the same trajectory, retain non-events as censored observations, and report warning availability, lead, tie, lag, and usable intervention time.

Habitat fragmentation provides a mechanistic setting in which these questions become inseparable. Reduced patch area and isolation can alter interaction rates, local population size, dispersal, drift, inbreeding, and selection. Eco-evolutionary work has shown that connectivity can produce rescue, migration load, erosion of local adaptation, or little effect depending on the life cycle and landscape (Legrand et al. 2017; Govaert et al. 2019). Yet fragmentation, genetic monitoring, and early-warning studies rarely follow a single causal sequence from patch geometry through interaction-dependent trait stability and local effective size to genetic diversity and realised functional-trait loss. As a result, a change in genetic diversity may be labelled a warning without establishing which ecological state it warns about or whether the warning remains portable across alternative genetic processes.

A particularly neglected process is the direction of recurrent genetic or effective state transitions. Evolutionary-rescue theory demonstrates that persistence under deterioration depends on population size, standing variation, mutation supply, dispersal, and the rate of environmental change (Gomulkiewicz & Holt 1995; Bell & Gonzalez 2009; Carlson et al. 2014; Bell 2017). Mutation-bias theory further shows that asymmetries in the introduction of variants can influence evolutionary outcomes through interactions with selection and finite-population sampling (Stoltzfus & McCandlish 2017; Storz et al. 2019; Svensson & Berger 2019; Cano et al. 2023). These literatures mainly ask whether adaptation or rescue occurs, not whether directional recurrent variation changes the existence of an evaluable trait-loss regime or the reliability and lead time of a genetic warning before functional loss.

Here we connect these previously separate problems in a finite eco-genetic framework. The predecessor model distinguished potential high-trait viability, realised trait occupancy, allele persistence, local effective population size, and genetic diversity. It linked habitat geometry and interaction feedback to finite genetic dynamics and showed, in one independently calibrated symmetric-mutation configuration, that baseline-relative erosion of alpha and gamma diversity preceded realised high-trait loss. That result provided a benchmark rather than a universal warning rule.

We extend the framework by separating the relaxation strength of recurrent variation from its directional equilibrium. This allows the total contraction of the mutation map to be held fixed while changing the relative recurrent transition toward high-trait-associated or low-trait-associated states. We ask four questions. First, does transition direction alter the feasibility of reconstructing and projecting a high-trait source? Second, does it partition deterioration experiments into rapid-loss, persistent, and seed-heterogeneous regimes? Third, can that partition eliminate the common calibration domain needed for an unbiased warning comparison? Fourth, after independent coordinate-specific calibration, does direction alter warning availability, lead/tie/lag ordering, censoring, and usable lead time?

Our design separates source reconstruction, trait-loss-only calibration, and warning validation with independent seed families. Calibration cannot inspect diversity or warning outcomes, and unavailable or non-event trajectories remain explicitly censored. This design tests a broader hypothesis than whether genetic diversity can decline before trait loss: genetic-warning reliability is an emergent property of the complete eco-genetic closure. The ecological implication is that genetic thresholds are not portable statistics. Their usefulness depends on the interaction state, spatial structure, finite-population dynamics, and recurrent processes that generate and remove functional states.
