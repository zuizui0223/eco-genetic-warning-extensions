# Competing eco-genetic pathways govern functional vulnerability under fragmentation

## Abstract

Fragmentation can leave ecological components present while changing the relations through which they sustain function. We combine exact results and prospectively locked finite experiments to identify why matched ecological and genetic marginals can lead to different futures. A fixed-area fragmentation gradient first separates potential viability, realised occupancy, interaction support, effective size and realised trait mass. We then show constructively that two states with identical census, interaction, trait and allele-frequency marginals, `H_alpha`, `H_gamma` and `F_ST` can have different exact next transitions. At fixed mean support, cross-layer covariance changes spatial support variance 49-fold. A new prospectively locked factorial intervention shows, however, that long-horizon fate is not a monotone function of alignment: trait and allele organization interact strongly, and mismatched trait–allele states increase functional-loss risk by 6.23 and 4.70 percentage points at generations 20 and 40. Removing direct eco-genetic feedback into interactions exposes a separate ~7-point matching advantage, demonstrating competition between matching-dependent recruitment and feedback-mediated compensation. Functional vulnerability is therefore generated by interacting relational pathways, not component amounts or a single alignment score.

Ecological fragmentation changes more than habitat amount. It can alter local demographic support, partner interactions, mating opportunity, movement, trait expression and genetic composition, and those quantities need not respond on the same schedule. The central ecological problem is therefore not simply how much of each component remains, but how the components required for a focal function are organized and coupled through the life cycle.

This distinction matters because numerical persistence can conceal functional vulnerability. Ecological interactions may become ineffective before species disappear, while movement or behavioural reorganization can sometimes compensate local resource loss. Genetic summaries can likewise retain historical information after contemporary interaction or mating processes have changed. These possibilities suggest that fragmentation may change not only component abundance but the **relational pathways** through which ecological and genetic states produce future function.

Existing theory provides pieces of this problem. Critical-transition theory formalizes nonlinear state changes; eco-evolutionary theory in fragmented landscapes couples demography, dispersal, selection and inheritance; multilayer ecology shows that interlayer organization can matter beyond layer-wise summaries; and forecasting theory emphasizes explicit targets and horizons. A less explicit question is mechanistic: **when two systems retain the same marginal ecological and genetic quantities, what relational structure makes their futures diverge?**

Natural systems motivate this question without serving as validation data for the finite model developed here. In urban *Crepis sancta*, local interaction limitation is associated with reproductive loss despite nonzero wider movement. On Miyake-jima, reduced floral resources can instead be accompanied by broader *Zosterops* movement and pollen mixing that compensate pollination. In fragmented *Conospermum undulatum*, contemporary pollen connectivity can deteriorate while adult neutral genetics retain an older landscape signature. These contrasting routes motivate the possibility that `urban`, `island` or `fragmented` describe upstream histories, whereas ecological fate depends on the processes through which local states are matched, mismatched or compensated.

We test that idea in four steps. First, we ask whether one fragmentation gradient generates one biological deterioration state. Second, we formalize transition sufficiency and identify the exact source of the immediate aligned/anti-aligned transition difference. Third, in a new prospectively locked factorial intervention, we separate trait alignment, allele alignment and direct eco-genetic feedback into the interaction state to identify why relational differences propagate—or fail to propagate—to long-horizon functional loss. Fourth, we ask whether an early marginal diversity response identifies functional fate on the full event and non-event denominator. Natural examples enter only after these tests, as ecological projections of the mechanisms rather than as external validation.

## Results

### Fragmentation separated functional support from persistence

The parent framework begins with a canonical positive-feedback interaction map

\[
q_{t+1}=\operatorname{sigmoid}\left[\kappa\left(\frac{A}{A_{\rm ref}}q_t-\theta\right)\right].
\]

Writing `K = kappa A/A_ref`, the map has a unique fixed point when `K <= 4`. For `K > 4`, two turning points define an exact open interval of `theta` with two locally stable branches separated by an unstable branch. This exact geometry applies to the declared map rather than to fragmented ecosystems universally.

We linked this interaction geometry to a finite trait–allele closure in which interaction state, potential high-trait viability, realised trait occupancy, effective size and genetic diversity remain distinct biological objects. A preregistered fixed-area fragmentation gradient projected the same 1,037 independently prepared high-state sources into 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 16 isolated equal patches.

The response was not one-dimensional. Potential high-trait viability was present in 1,037/1,037 supported one-patch outcomes but absent in 1,037/1,037 outcomes at every tested subdivision. Yet realised high-trait occupancy persisted at generation 30 in approximately 99.6–100% of supported trajectories. At the first split into two patches, median retained interaction was 0.001744 of the one-patch value, local effective size was 0.221311 and realised high-trait mass was 0.282918. Interaction and effective size continued to decline with patch count, whereas realised high-trait mass partially recovered, reaching 0.393880 of its one-patch reference at sixteen patches.

Thus numerical occupancy could persist after the declared interaction environment supporting potential high-trait function had changed sharply. Fragmentation separated biological states rather than moving one latent deterioration coordinate.

### Cross-layer covariance controlled the immediate transition at fixed marginal state

Let `X` be the complete explicit present state and let

\[
\phi:X\rightarrow Z
\]

be a coarse representation containing habitat area, patch and total census, interaction, trait and allele-frequency marginals, complete trait-bin totals, `H_alpha`, `H_gamma` and `F_ST`. Let

\[
T_I:X\rightarrow Q
\]

be the exact one-generation interaction transition. We call `phi` transition-sufficient for `T_I` if there exists a map `g` such that `T_I=g\circ\phi` for every admissible state under the declared closure.

We constructed two states with identical `phi(X)` while reversing the spatial association between interaction state and a common trait/allele support bundle. Cross-layer covariance changed from `+0.025` to `-0.025`, and the exact generation-1 interaction fields differed by as much as **0.2543**. Because `phi(X_A)=phi(X_B)` but `T_I(X_A) != T_I(X_B)`, no transition map defined only on the retained marginals can reproduce both next states.

The mechanism of that immediate difference is exact. Local interaction support is

\[
S_j=\alpha q_j+\beta T_j+\gamma G_j,
\]

with `(alpha,beta,gamma)=(0.6,0.3,0.1)`. In the original construction `T_j=G_j=B_j`, so

\[
S_j=0.6q_j+0.4B_j.
\]

Both states have the same mean support, `0.68`. Their spatial support distributions are nevertheless `.47,.61,.75,.89` and `.71,.69,.67,.65`, giving population variances `0.0245` and `0.0005`: a **49-fold difference at identical mean support**. More generally,

\[
\mathrm{Var}(S)=\alpha^2\mathrm{Var}(q)+(\beta+\gamma)^2\mathrm{Var}(B)
+2\alpha(\beta+\gamma)\mathrm{Cov}(q,B),
\]

so, once the marginals are fixed, cross-layer covariance changes the spatial variance of local support directly. The immediate state difference is therefore relational in a precise sense: covariance changes where support is concentrated, not how much average support exists.

A previously locked 1,500-pair propagation experiment showed that this relational distinction could be consequential under one declared deterioration path, with anti-aligned minus aligned functional-loss risk differences of `+5.33` and `+5.20` percentage points at generations 20 and 40. We next asked prospectively whether that direction represented a stable mechanism or one realization of competing pathways.

### Long-horizon fate emerged from competing recruitment and compensation pathways

Before opening new outcomes, we locked a six-condition mechanism experiment. Four full-feedback conditions crossed trait assignment (`aligned` or `reversed`) with allele assignment (`aligned` or `reversed`): `AA`, `AR`, `RA` and `RR`. Two additional conditions, `AA_q-only` and `RR_q-only`, removed direct trait and allele contributions to the interaction-support signal by changing its weights from `(0.6,0.3,0.1)` to `(1,0,0)`. All other state values, forcing, horizons and endpoint definitions were fixed. Each condition used 1,500 trajectories in the same paired seed blocks.

The new full-feedback ensemble did **not** reproduce a stable directional `AA`–`RR` long-horizon difference. At generation 20, `RR-AA` loss risk was `-0.8` percentage points (95% CI `-4.16` to `+2.56`); at generation 40 it was `+0.6` points (`-2.69` to `+3.89`). The earlier locked `+5`-point propagation therefore cannot be interpreted as a universal rule that one orientation of alignment is protective.

The factorial decomposition instead revealed strong non-additivity between trait and allele organization. At generation 20 the loss rates were `0.3733`, `0.4520`, `0.4113` and `0.3653` for `AA`, `AR`, `RA` and `RR`; at generation 40 they were `0.6720`, `0.7407`, `0.7033` and `0.6780`. The paired trait-by-allele interaction contrast was **-12.47 percentage points** at generation 20 (95% CI `-16.30` to `-8.63`) and **-9.40 points** at generation 40 (`-13.01` to `-5.79`). Equivalently, the two trait–allele mismatched cells (`AR`,`RA`) had average loss risk **6.23 points** higher than the two matched cells (`AA`,`RR`) at generation 20 (`+4.32` to `+8.15`) and **4.70 points** higher at generation 40 (`+2.90` to `+6.50`). The main effects of reversing either layer alone were much smaller. Long-horizon vulnerability therefore depended strongly on **coherence between trait and allele layers**, not on a scalar alignment orientation.

The `q-only` intervention identified a second pathway. Because direct trait and allele feedback into `q` was removed, `AA_q-only` and `RR_q-only` had exactly the same generation-1 interaction field and initial q-support signal. Yet later functional loss diverged: `RR-AA` was **+7.13 percentage points** at generation 20 (`+3.91` to `+10.36`) and **+6.93 points** at generation 40 (`+3.79` to `+10.08`). At generation 20, aligned states also retained `0.0730` more upper-tail high-trait mass (`+0.0455` to `+0.1005`) and `0.0847` more realised high-trait refugia on average (`+0.0361` to `+0.1332`). The direction persisted at generation 40.

Thus direct trait/allele feedback into interaction state is **not necessary** for a relational effect to reach functional loss. The parent life cycle supplies the indirect path. High allele frequency sets the mixture of high- and low-trait recruitment kernels; resident trait state enters through the inheritance mixture; recruits are selected by interaction-dependent trait fitness; and high allele state also affects demographic growth, whose density enters later interaction updates. A high-trait/high-allele bundle located where interaction conditions favour the high trait therefore receives mutually compatible recruitment and selection. The same coherent bundle placed in a weak-interaction patch is ecologically mismatched.

Full feedback introduces an opposing effect. Trait and allele state themselves contribute directly to the local interaction-support signal. Placing a strong eco-genetic bundle in a weak-interaction patch can therefore raise local `q`, partially repairing the mismatch. The new results identify **two competing relational pathways**: a matching-dependent recruitment pathway that favours co-location with a suitable ecological state, and a feedback-mediated compensation pathway that strengthens weak local interaction states. Their net balance, rather than alignment itself, determines long-horizon fate.

### Early genetic erosion did not identify functional fate

Relational mechanisms can shape future function without making every marginal variable a useful warning. We tested six frozen baseline-relative diversity rules: 5%, 10% and 20% declines in `H_alpha` or `H_gamma`.

Every rule preceded all 35 observed losses in the inherited ensemble and all 33 losses in an independently seeded fresh ensemble. But restoring the full baseline-eligible denominator showed that the same rules also fired in all 48 inherited non-event trajectories and all 49 fresh non-events by the common horizon. Every frozen rule therefore had sensitivity `1`, false-positive rate `1`, specificity `0` and binary-marker AUC `0.5` in both ensembles.

The separation follows exactly from the denominator. If `Y=1` denotes loss by the horizon and `M=1` marker firing, perfect event-conditioned precedence fixes sensitivity at one but places no restriction on firing among `Y=0` trajectories. If `f` of `n_0` non-events fire,

\[
\mathrm{specificity}=\frac{n_0-f}{n_0},
\qquad
\mathrm{AUC}=\frac{1+\mathrm{specificity}}{2}
\]

for a binary horizon marker. Perfect event-side ordering is therefore compatible with binary AUC from `0.5` to `1.0`. The observed rules occupy the lower endpoint because every non-event fired.

The result does not imply that genetic diversity is ecologically irrelevant. It shows that a marginal variable can be stress-sensitive and temporally early while failing to discriminate among trajectories whose relational pathways lead to different functional fates.

## Discussion

The mechanistic result changes the ecological interpretation of fragmentation. Functional vulnerability is relational, but **relation is not one scalar axis**. The immediate transition difference between matched-marginal states is exactly attributable to covariance changing the spatial variance of local support. Long-horizon fate, however, depends on how several relational pathways interact across the life cycle. Trait–allele coherence promotes consistent recruitment, ecological matching determines whether those recruits occur where the focal trait is favoured, and feedback can compensate mismatch by strengthening weak interaction states.

This explains why the new independent full-feedback ensemble did not reproduce a simple directional `AA`–`RR` loss difference even though the exact next-state difference is deterministic. Direct interaction feedback partially repairs the very spatial mismatch that is disadvantageous for recruitment. Removing that compensation exposes the underlying matching advantage; breaking trait–allele coherence produces an additional penalty. The same relational rearrangement can therefore generate opposing effects at different points in the causal chain.

This is more informative than saying that “alignment matters.” It predicts a distinction between **uncompensated mismatch** and **feedback-mediated recoupling**. Where ecological context, genetic recruitment and trait state are mismatched and no process rebuilds their local compatibility, functional loss should accelerate. Where movement, partner reorganization or other feedbacks restore effective local conditions, the same initial disturbance may be compensated. Temporal lag adds a third possibility when standing genetic state records an earlier configuration after current functional processes have changed.

The natural literature provides examples of these possibilities without validating the finite model. In urban *Crepis sancta*, reduced local flowering density is associated with reduced pollinator activity and reproduction despite wider movement, illustrating limited compensation. Miyake-jima *Camellia–Zosterops* provides the opposite projection: reduced floral resources are accompanied by broader bird movement and pollen mixing, consistent with feedback-mediated recoupling. *Conospermum undulatum* illustrates temporal mismatch between current pollen processes and adult genetic memory, whereas *Spondias purpurea* shows a more coordinated deterioration across visitation, pollen flow, reproductive function and younger-cohort genetics. These systems motivate candidate pathways; they are not replications of the model.

The same interpretation clarifies the earlier urban–island comparison. `Urban` and `island` are upstream histories, not mechanistic states. They can differ in partner mobility, rewiring, mating opportunity, reproductive assurance and genetic memory, all of which determine whether mismatch is reinforced or compensated. Existing Honshu–Izu and Zurich archives cannot identify a universal shared urban–island law because their state coordinates and endpoints are not harmonized. A prospective comparison should instead ask which relational pathways—matching, compensation or memory—are active and whether they explain future function beyond the landscape label.

Our separate natural-data measurement programme reaches a complementary boundary. Candidate process states can fail because a relevant coordinate is absent, a proxy does not earn endpoint relevance, or preprocessing erases the relation it was meant to measure. Those analyses remain non-load-bearing here. Their value is to specify an end-to-end empirical design: synchronize local resource and demographic support, realised interactions, movement or mating connectivity, trait and cohort-specific genetic state, and later function in the same held-out populations. Crucially, the new mechanism suggests measuring not only each coordinate but also whether compensatory processes change the local match among them.

The warning result becomes a monitoring consequence of the same logic. Marginal diversity erosion may reveal system-wide stress while missing whether recruitment mismatch is being amplified or compensated. A useful warning for functional collapse therefore needs to discriminate **pathway state**, not merely detect that one component has begun to erode.

Together, the results suggest a new organizing question for fragmentation ecology: not simply how much habitat, abundance or diversity remains, but **which relational pathway currently controls the production of function?** Collapse, compensation and lag need not be contradictory responses to fragmentation. They can emerge when matching-dependent recruitment, feedback-mediated recoupling and historical memory dominate to different degrees.

The claim is deliberately bounded. The finite closure does not establish a universal alignment score, a universal protective effect of heterogeneity, or a natural generation-20 threshold. Indeed, the prospective decomposition rejects such a simplification. What it establishes is stronger mechanistically: identical marginal states can differ immediately because covariance redistributes support, and their later fate can change direction because distinct ecological and genetic pathways oppose one another.

Functional vulnerability under fragmentation is therefore a property of **competing relational pathways**. Components may persist, marginal diversity may remain measurable, and an early signal may be reproducible, while the decisive mechanism lies in whether ecological conditions, recruitment and feedback maintain—or reconstruct—the local combinations that generate function.

## Methods

### Evidence architecture

The integrated analysis uses four load-bearing evidence blocks. The fragmentation/state-separation results are inherited from the theorem-guided parent framework. The exact transition-sufficiency counterexample and original propagation experiment are owned by the state-validity programme. The new relational mechanism decomposition was prospectively locked after those results and before its outcomes were opened. The full-denominator warning result is owned by the frozen warning-validity audit. Natural examples and natural-data audits are used only for ecological interpretation and prospective measurement design.

### Canonical interaction-state theorem and finite fragmentation gradient

For the canonical map, fixed points satisfy

\[
\operatorname{logit}(q)-Kq+\kappa\theta=0,
\qquad K=\kappa A/A_{\rm ref}.
\]

Because the derivative is `1/[q(1-q)]-K` and `1/[q(1-q)] >= 4` on `(0,1)`, strict bistability requires `K>4`; the exact barrier interval is obtained from the two turning points. The finite fragmentation experiment used 1,200 attempted fresh source preparations, of which 1,037 completed the required high-state preparation. Each supported source was projected into 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 or 16 isolated equal patches under fixed total area and the declared full-state preservation contract.

### Transition-sufficiency counterexample and original propagation

The coarse representation `phi` and exact transition `T_I` are defined in the Results. The aligned/anti-aligned constructive pair holds all declared marginals fixed and reverses the cross-layer association. The exact generation-1 transition was evaluated before long-horizon outcomes. A later protocol, independently locked before extension outcomes, followed 1,500 paired trajectories once to generation 40 and read cumulative functional loss at generations 5, 10, 20 and 40.

### Prospective relational mechanism decomposition

The mechanism protocol fixed six conditions before outcomes were opened. Four full-feedback states crossed ascending or reversed trait assignment with ascending or reversed allele assignment (`AA`, `AR`, `RA`, `RR`) while preserving all layer-wise marginals. Two `q-only` states (`AA_q-only`, `RR_q-only`) set the interaction-support weights to `(1,0,0)`, removing direct trait and allele contributions to `q` while retaining the same recruitment, selection and demographic closure. All conditions used the same four-patch initial interaction state, rising barrier schedule, no migration, five master seeds and 300 replicates per seed, for 1,500 trajectories per condition. The endpoint remained first all-patch loss of realised high-trait occupancy.

Primary predeclared outputs included support variance, upper-tail support, maximum interaction state, realised high-trait refuge count and paired loss risk at generations 20 and 40. Standard paired factorial contrasts were computed within each master-seed/replicate block. No additional alignment permutations, weights, barrier schedules, endpoints or horizons were opened after the results.

### Warning denominator audit

The six frozen markers were the first post-baseline generations at which `H_alpha` or `H_gamma` declined 5%, 10% or 20% from its own baseline. The inherited ensemble retained 83 baseline-eligible trajectories (35 losses, 48 horizon non-events); the independently seeded fresh ensemble retained 82 (33 losses, 49 non-events). Thresholds, eligibility, horizon and event definition were not recalibrated. The audit restored all baseline-eligible trajectories and calculated sensitivity, specificity, false-positive rate, predictive values and binary-marker AUC.

### Natural projection boundary

Published natural systems are cited as ecological examples only. The separate natural-data measurement analyses are not treated as external validation of the finite simulator. No cross-system ecological effect is pooled and no urban–island equality is estimated from unmatched archives.
